Student Performance Assessment Rubric
Computer Programming 1  |  Week 1 pilot scope  |  FOR INSTRUCTOR VALIDATION
1  Purpose, scope and outputs
This instrument provides a reproducible 0–100 gameplay assessment score for each evaluated programming challenge and a descriptive Week 1 summary. It measures observable performance on the stated Computer Programming 1 learning objective; it is NOT itself the adaptive difficulty label, a standardized proficiency test, or an official course grade.
Scope: Week 1 prototype, ten planned levels. The same assessment framework may be reused across later regions after content-specific review. Exact Week 1 syllabus objectives and approved challenge exemplars must be attached by the instructor before deployment.
2  Proposed scoring model (100 points)
Criterion
Max
Operational scoring rule
A. Functional correctness
70
70 × passed predefined scored test cases ÷ total scored test cases. Use equal weights unless a documented, instructor-approved weighting applies.
B. Concept application
20
20 × satisfied objective-specific, observable concept checks ÷ total approved checks. Checks must measure evidence not duplicated by output tests.
C. Required deliverables
10
10 × completed nonduplicative required artifacts ÷ total specified artifacts (e.g., required code file or explicitly requested explanation).

Overall challenge score = A + B + C (round once to one decimal place; range 0–100). Define all denominators and scoring items before gameplay. If a criterion is inapplicable, the instructor must approve and document a revised weighting before use—not automatically award full marks.
Scoring prerequisites and evidence
For every generated activity, require: challenge ID, syllabus objective, prerequisite, intended difficulty, complete prompt, reference solution, executable or verifiable test cases with expected outputs, concept-check list, required deliverables, and a scoring version. Flag ambiguous or non-executable activities for review rather than assigning a made-up grade.
Where code is executable, use sandboxed, time-limited evaluation and validate test cases before use. If an activity is conceptual or multiple-choice, use a separately instructor-approved modality-specific scoring key; do not force code-test formulas onto non-code challenges.

Mistake count, retries, hint usage, speed, and first-try success remain recorded as separate gameplay indicators for adaptive difficulty; they do not reduce the academic-style correctness score automatically. This avoids penalizing students simply for using legitimate learning support.
4  Attempt policy and Week 1 summary
Primary challenge score: use the first evaluated submission after a student has been shown the challenge and before corrective feedback or a worked solution is revealed. Record any subsequent best/last assisted score separately. A challenge exited without a scored submission is recorded as not assessed (missing), not silently scored zero; any institutional policy for non-submission must be decided explicitly.
Level score: use the designated primary assessment activity for that level. Other generated variants are practice/reinforcement, recorded separately and not averaged as additional levels. If an activity is invalidated by content review, issue an approved replacement and preserve the audit trail.
Week 1 descriptive score = arithmetic mean of the ten valid primary level scores, only when all ten required levels have valid scores. For incomplete participation, report the number assessed and partial mean separately; do not present a partial mean as a completed weekly grade.
Because student challenges differ in difficulty, raw weekly averages are descriptive in-game scores; they should not be treated as directly equivalent measures of underlying programming mastery without common anchor items or validated equating. A common test is preferred for between-student learning comparisons.

6  Review and deployment conditions
The CP1/fundamentals instructor should verify criterion relevance, absence of duplicate credit, suitable difficulty, test coverage, accessibility, and consistency across examples. Pilot-test automated scoring against instructor-scored samples, document discrepancies, and lock the scoring version for research collection.
