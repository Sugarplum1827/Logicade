CHAPTER I 
The Problem and Its Background 
Background of the Study 
The growing presence of data in nearly every aspect of modern life 
has made the ability to analyze and interpret it an increasingly essential skill, 
particularly in fields such as computer science, information technology, and 
engineering. Students and prac titioners alike are expected to work with 
datasets of varying sizes and complexities, applying computational methods 
to extract patterns, generate predictions, and support informed decisions. 
At the heart of these processes are machine learning algorithms,  
mathematical models that learn from data to perform classification, 
regression, clustering, and other analytical tasks. As the range of available 
algorithms continues to expand, so too does the difficulty of determining 
which one is most appropriate for a given dataset and problem, a challenge 
that sits at the center of what this study seeks to address. 
Choosing the right machine learning algorithm is far from 
straightforward. Different algorithms are designed with different 
assumptions about data, and their performance varies considerably 
depending on the characteristics of the dataset being used, includ ing how 
large it is, how many features it contains, whether the classes are balanced, 
and how the values are distributed. A student or researcher who simply 
applies the first familiar algorithm to a dataset without considering these 
properties of risks obtaining results that are inaccurate, misleading, or 
difficult to interpret. In academic settings, this problem is compounded by 

[PAGE 3]
 
 
 
 
the fact that most learners do not yet have the depth of experience needed 
to make confident, informed choices. The typical response is to try multiple 
algorithms one at a time, compare results, and select whichever performs 
best, an approach that is slow,  inefficient, and does not build genuine 
understanding of why one algorithm outperforms another. 
This experience of uncertainty and inefficiency in algorithm selection 
is a widely recognized challenge in undergraduate data science education. 
Classroom instruction in most higher education institutions covers how 
algorithms work in theory but rarely pro vides structured, systematic 
guidance on how to match an algorithm to the specific properties of a real -
world dataset. The result is a significant disconnect between conceptual 
knowledge and practical application, one that hinders students from 
engaging me aningfully with machine learning beyond surface -level 
familiarity. Undergraduate students, particularly those introductory data 
science and computer science courses, often resort to trial-and-error rather 
than principled decision-making when confronted with unfamiliar datasets. 
This gap is further widened by the nature of existing tools designed 
to automate parts of the machine learning pipeline. Most currently available 
automated machine learning (AutoML) platforms, such as Auto -sklearn, 
AutoGluon, and GAMA, are built primarily for professional data scientists 
and presuppose a level of technical expertise that undergraduate students 
have not yet gained. These tools often produce results without explanation, 
leaving users with little understanding o f why a particular algorithm was 
recommended or how the dataset's characteristics influenced that 

[PAGE 4]
 
 
 
 
recommendation. For a student who is still building foundational knowledge, 
this kind of black -box output offers limited educational value. What is 
needed is not simply automation, but guided, interpretable automation, a 
system that not only recommends an algorithm but also helps the user 
understand the reasoning behind that recommendation. 
This need is particularly evident in the Philippine higher education 
context, where Filipino undergraduate students working with structured 
educational and institutional datasets frequently encounter the same 
algorithm selection challenges, and where the c hoice of machine learning 
algorithm has measurable consequences on the quality of analytical 
outcomes. Despite this, no locally developed tool currently exists that 
provides undergraduate students with an accessible, dataset-informed, and 
educationally mea ningful pathway to algorithm selection. The disconnect 
between the growing demand for data literacy in Philippine academia and 
the inaccessibility of existing tools represents a clear gap that this study 
aims to address. 
It is from this recognition that SimuRithm was conceptualized. 
SimuRithm is an intelligent decision support system that takes an uploaded 
dataset, analyzes its structural and statistical properties, and recommends 
the most suitable machine learning algorithm based on what it finds. It uses 
Natural Language Processing (NLP) tec hniques, specifically tokenization 
and lexical analysis via NLTK, to interpret column headers and infer what 
each variable represents. It performs descriptive and correlation analyses 
to characterize the dataset and employs a meta -learning model trained on 

[PAGE 5]
 
 
 
 
algorithm-dataset performance histories to generate its recommendations. 
The system is designed from the ground up with undergraduate students in 
mind, presenting its outputs in clear, readable formats, offering step-by-step 
guidance, and providing visuali zations that make data characteristics 
tangible and understandable. 
SimuRithm does not aim to replace the learning process. Rather, it 
aims to support it, giving students a structured starting point from which they 
can explore their data, understand its properties, and make informed 
decisions about how to analyze it. By embedding intelligence into a tool that 
is genuinely accessible to beginners, SimuRithm  seeks to bridge the gap 
between theoretical instruction and practical application that many students 
in Philippine higher education currently face. The development of this 
system is grounded in the belief that when students are given the right tools 
and the right guidance, they become more confident, more capable, and 
more genuinely engaged with the field of machine learning. 
Despite the increasing availability of machine learning tools, most 
existing platforms are designed for experienced practitioners and require 
substantial technical knowledge to operate effectively. Students and 
beginners in data science often struggle to identify which machine learning 
algorithm is most appropriate for  their dataset, resulting in inefficient trial -
and-error approaches, wasted time, and poor model outcomes. There is a 
clear need for an accessible, intelligent system that can automatically 
analyze dataset characteristics and guide users toward the most su itable 
algorithm with minimal technical burden.

Statement of the Problem 
This study aims to develop SimuRithm, an intelligent decision support 
system that assists students in identifying the uploaded datasets, processes 
text-related information, evaluates all applicable algorithms in its extensible 
algorithm repository, and displays an output for every major proc ess step. 
Specifically, the study seeks to answer the following questions:  
1. How can the system effectively accept, parse, and validate 
heterogeneous datasets (incorporating numerical, categorical, 
alphanumeric, and text -based attributes) from diverse formats like 
CSV and Excel, while leveraging Natural Language Processing 
(NLP) on metadata and text fields to automate variable identification 
and feature extraction?  
2. How can an extensible algorithm repository be designed and 
integrated to dynamically support, execute, and benchmark a wide 
spectrum of machine learning tasks, specifically encompassing the 
following: 
a. Classification 
b. Regression 
c. Clustering 
d. Anomaly Detection 
e. Dimensionality Reduction 
f. Topic Modeling 
g. Text Similarity 
h. Sequence Modeling  

[PAGE 7]
 
 
 
 
3. How can a hybrid meta -learning or rule -assisted recommendation 
engine analyze dataset characteristics, NLP -derived features, and 
empirical execution performance to intelligently rank and recommend 
the most suitable algorithms based on user-selected priorities?  
4. What granular outputs must be displayed at each major operational 
stage (from upload to export ) to ensure transparency, and to what 
extent does the resulting SimuRithm  system comply with ISO/IEC 
25010 software quality standards while reducing the trial and -error 
burden for undergraduate students? 
Objectives of the Study 
The general objective of this study is to design and develop 
SimuRithm as an NLP -assisted intelligent decision support system for 
comprehensive machine learning algorithm selection with visible step -by-
step output generation. Specifically, this study aims to: 
1. Develop a system capable of accepting, parsing, validating, and 
processing CSV and Excel datasets containing numerical, 
categorical, alphanumeric, and text -based attributes, while utilizing 
Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques such as 
tokenization, normalization, lexical analysis, vectorization, and 
optional embedding methods for metadata interpretation, variable 
identification, and feature extraction. 
2. Design and integrate an extensible algorithm repository that 
dynamically supports, executes, and benchmarks machine learning 
tasks including classification, regression, clustering, anomaly 

[PAGE 8]
 
 
 
 
detection, dimensionality reduction, topic modeling, text similarity, 
and sequence modeling using appropriate performance evaluation 
metrics. 
3. Develop a recommendation engine that analyzes dataset 
characteristics, NLP -derived feature characteristics, task type, 
algorithm performance metrics, and user -selected priorities to rank 
and recommend the most suitable machine learning algorithms. 
4. Create a transparent user interface that displays outputs at every 
major stage of the process —from dataset upload and validation to 
preprocessing, benchmarking, recommendation, visualization, and 
export—and evaluate the resulting system using ISO/IEC 25010  
software quality standards and user feedback to determine its 
effectiveness in reducing trial-and-error in algorithm selection. 
Theoretical Framework 
This study is based on three main theories that explain the design 
and purpose of the SimuRithm  system. These theories are the No Free 
Lunch Theorem, Meta -learning Theory, and Decision Support System 
Theory. Together, they support the system’s goal of helping students 
choose the most suitable machine learning algorithm for a given dataset. 
The diagram below shows how each theory connects to the main parts. 

[PAGE 9]
 
 
 
 
 
Figure 1.1. Theoretical Diagram 
The theoretical framework of SimuRithm  is based on the No Free 
Lunch Theorem, Meta -learning Theory, and Decision Support System 
Theory. The No Free Lunch Theorem explains that no single machine 
learning algorithm performs best for every dataset, since each dataset has 
different characteristics  such as size, features, distribution, and variable 
relationships. Because of this, SimuRithm  first analyzes the uploaded 
dataset before recommending an algorithm. Meta -learning Theory, also 
known as “learning to learn,” supports this process by using past results 
from different datasets to help predict which algorithm may be suitable for a 
new dataset. In SimuRithm, this is applied through the analysis of dataset 
features, descriptive statistics, correlation results, and other relevant 
dataset information. Lastly,  the Decision Support System Theory explains 
that a system should assist users in making decisions rather than replacing 
their judgment. SimuRithm follows this idea by presenting the 


[PAGE 10]
 
 
 
 
recommended algorithm together with statistical results, visualizations, and 
explanations that help students understand the basis of the 
recommendation. Through these theories, SimuRithm  is supported as an 
intelligent decision support system that helps undergraduate students and 
beginner data analysts reduce trial -and-error, understand their datasets, 
and make informed decisions in selecting machine learning algorithms, 
while also serving  as an additional teaching tool for educators and 
professors. 
 
Conceptual Framework 
 


[PAGE 11]
 
 
 
 
Figure 1.2. Conceptual Framework 
This diagram presents the overall process of SimuRithm : An 
Intelligent Decision Support System for Automated ML Model Selection. The 
framework follows an Input –Process–Output (IPO) model, where the input 
consists of structured datasets in CSV or Excel format, dataset components, 
and user preferences such as m achine learning category and variable 
selection. 
The process begins with Dataset Understanding, where the system 
analyzes the uploaded dataset to determine its characteristics and 
structure. This is followed by Dataset Preparation, which transforms the data 
into a suitable format for machine learning execution. The ML Category 
Workflow identifies the appropriate machine learning task based on the 
dataset characteristics and selected variables. After identifying t he task 
category, the system performs Algorithm Benchmarking by evaluating 
suitable machine learning algorithms and comparing their performance. The 
system also undergoes evaluation to determine its effectiveness, usability, 
and reliability. 
The expected output of the system includes dataset insights, 
algorithm performance results, and intelligent recommendations. The 
framework demonstrates how SimuRithm assists users, particularly 
beginners, in understanding datasets and selecting suitable ma chine 
learning algorithms through a guided and data  driven decision support 
process.

Significance of the Study 
The development of SimuRithm carries significant value for multiple 
stakeholders in the academic and research communities. 
Students. As the primary beneficiary group, students stand to gain 
the most from the system. SimuRithm  reduces the complexity of selecting 
machine learning algorithms by automatically analyzing datasets and 
generating comprehensible outputs such as descriptive statistics, 
correlation results, and graphical visualizations. By providing guided, step -
by-step recommendations, the system helps students avoid common errors 
in data analysis and fosters a deeper understanding of the relationship 
between dataset properties and model performance. The customization 
feature further allows students to apply their own va riable selections, 
reinforcing active learning. 
Educators and Professors. Faculty members in Computer Science 
and Data Science programs can utilize SimuRithm as a supplementary 
teaching tool. The system's visual outputs and algorithm explanations can 
serve as discussion starting points for lessons on machine learning model 
selection, dataset preprocessing, and performance evaluation, reducing the 
need for extensive individual guidance during laboratory exercises. 
Academic Institutions.  The study contributes to the institution's 
commitment to technology -aided instruction and research innovation. By 
developing an intelligent system tailored for undergraduate use, the 
institution demonstrates its capacity to produce applied research that 
addresses real educational challenges in the local context. 

[PAGE 13]
 
 
 
 
Future Researchers.  This study establishes a foundation for 
subsequent research in automated machine learning, intelligent tutoring 
systems, and educational technology. Future researchers may extend 
SimuRithm's capabilities to support a wider range of algorithms, handle 
unstructured data types, or integrate adaptive learning features that 
personalize recommendations based on individual user history. 
Scope and Limitations of the Study 
This study focuses on the design, development, and evaluation of 
SimuRithm, an intelligent decision support system for automated machine 
learning model selection, intended primarily for academic use by 
undergraduate students who need a guide system for analyzing datasets 
and choosing suitable algorithms.  
The system accepts CSV and Excel datasets containing numerical, 
categorical, alphanumeric, and text -based attributes. It supports dataset 
parsing, validation, dataset overview generation, NLP -based header and 
text-field interpretation, variable role identification, task detection, 
preprocessing, feature engineering, algorithm filtering, benchmarking, 
recommendation, visualization, and report generation. 
The system uses an extensible algorithm repository. This study 
includes algorithms for classification, regression, clustering, anomaly 
detection, dimensionality reduction, topic modeling, text similarity, and 
sequence modeling when available in the implementation environment. 
The system must display a visible output for every major step. This 
includes upload status, validation report, dataset preview, column and data 

[PAGE 14]
 
 
 
 
type summary, missing value report, NLP tokenization and header 
interpretation output, detected text -field output, preprocessing log, 
generated feature summary, candidate algorithm output, algorithm 
exclusion reason, benchmark output, visual outputs, final  recommendation 
explanation, and downloadable result report. 
The study acknowledges several limitations. First, although the 
algorithm repository is designed to be comprehensive and extensible, it 
cannot literally contain every algorithm ever created. Its coverage depends 
on the algorithms implemented in the selected Python libraries and those 
encoded by the researchers. Second, deep learning and transformer-based 
methods may require greater computing resources and may be limited by 
available hardware. Third, system performance depends on the quality, 
size, and completeness of the uploaded dataset. Fourth, NLP interpretation 
may be affected by ambiguous column names, mixed languages, 
abbreviations, spelling errors, or domai n-specific terminology. Fifth, 
recommendations are decision-support outputs and should be reviewed by 
users before being used in formal research conclusions. Six, images, video, 
and audio files are beyond the scope of the study. 
Definition of Terms 
Algorithm Recommendation. The system output identifies and explains 
the most suitable algorithm or ranked set of algorithms based on dataset 
properties, NLP-derived features, task type, and performance results. 

[PAGE 15]
 
 
 
 
Algorithm Repository. The extensible collection of machine learning and 
NLP-compatible algorithms available for filtering, benchmarking, and 
recommendation within SimuRithm. 
Alphanumeric Data. Data composed of letters, numbers, or combinations 
of both, commonly found in structured datasets such as IDs, labels, codes, 
categories, and descriptive values. 
Candidate Algorithm Output. The visible list of algorithms that the system 
considers suitable for the detected task and data representation, including 
the reason for inclusion or exclusion. 
Classification. A machine learning task that predicts a categorical output 
or class label. 
Clustering. An unsupervised machine learning task that groups similar 
records without requiring a predefined target variable. 
Correlation Analysis.  A statistical method used to identify relationships 
among numerical variables in a dataset. 
Dataset. A collection of data organized in rows and columns and uploaded 
to the system in CSV or Excel format. 
Dataset Characteristics. Measurable properties of the uploaded dataset, 
including row count, column count, data types, missing values, class 
balance, distribution, sparsity, text length, vocabulary size, and feature 
dimensionality. 
Data Visualization. The graphical presentation of dataset characteristics, 
NLP outputs, model performance, and recommendation results through 
charts, tables, and diagrams. 

[PAGE 16]
 
 
 
 
Deep Learning Algorithm.  A machine learning method based on neural 
networks, such as multilayer perceptron, convolutional networks, recurrent 
networks, and transformer-based models. 
Dimensionality Reduction. A process of reducing the number of features 
while preserving useful information for analysis or modeling. 
Feature Engineering.  The process of transforming raw data into usable 
model inputs, including encoding categorical variables, scaling numeric 
values, and converting text into numerical representations. 
Meta-learning. A machine learning approach where a system learns from 
previous dataset and algorithm performance records to guide future 
recommendations. 
Natural Language Processing.  A branch of artificial intelligence that 
enables computers to process and analyze human language. In SimuRithm, 
NLP is used for header interpretation, text -field detection, tokenization, 
vectorization, and feature extraction. 
NLP-Derived Features.  Numerical representations produced from 
language data, such as token counts, n -grams, TF -IDF vectors, word 
embeddings, sentence embeddings, or transformer embeddings. 
Output Visibility. The system requires that every major step must display 
an understandable output to the user before proceeding to the next stage. 
Preprocessing Log.  A displayed record of cleaning and transformation 
actions applied to the dataset, including missing value handling, encoding, 
scaling, tokenization, vectorization, and train-test splitting. 
Regression. A machine learning task that predicts a continuous numerical 
output. 

[PAGE 17]
 
 
 
 
Task Detection.  The process of identifying whether the dataset is most 
appropriate for classification, regression, clustering, topic modeling, 
similarity search, anomaly detection, or another supported task. 
Text Vectorization.  The conversion of text into numerical form so that 
machine learning algorithms can process it. 
Tokenization. The NLP process of breaking text or column headers into 
smaller meaningful units called tokens. 
Transformer-Based Model.  A neural language model architecture that 
uses attention mechanisms and can be applied to tasks such as text 
classification, semantic similarity, and language understanding.

Synthesis 
 The body of literature and studies reviewed across both foreign and 
local sources converges on three overarching themes that collectively justify 
the development of SimuRithm: (1) the context -dependence of machine 
learning algorithm performance, (2) the pe rsistent accessibility gap in 
existing automated machine learning tools, and (3) the growing but 
underserved demand for data -driven educational guidance systems within 
the Philippine academic context. 
The foreign literature consistently demonstrates that no single 
machine learning algorithm achieves optimal performance across all 
dataset configurations. Uddin and Lu (2024) empirically established that 
both meta -level and statistical dataset properties s erve as reliable 
determinants of classifier performance across two hundred tabular datasets, 
whereas Garouani  et al. (2023) demonstrated that such dataset 
characteristics may be encoded as meta-features and mapped to the most 
appropriate algorithm. Dagan et al. (2024), Tornede et al. (2023), and Vente 
and Beel (2025) further established that meta -learning frameworks trained 
on prior algorithm –dataset performance histories are capable of 

[PAGE 46]
 
 
 
 
generalizing recommendations to previously unseen datasets, thereby 
validating the core decision logic underlying SimuRithm. 
The local literature reinforces this context -dependence within the 
Philippine setting. Salvador (2024) reported that, among five boosting 
algorithms applied to Philippine household data, CatBoost achieved 
markedly superior performance relative to the alternatives, while Francisco 
et al. (2024) demonstrated that a hybrid approach exceeded the accuracy 
of single models in dengue prediction across Metropolitan Manila, and 
Buebos-Esteve and Dagamac  (2024) observed that the best -performing 
learner was contingent upon the task and clustering strategy employed. 
Švábenský et al. (2024), in modeling the academic performance of Filipino 
students, similarly found that model behavior and fairness varied with the 
characteristics of the underlying data. Collectively, these findings confirm 
that intelligent, dataset -sensitive alg orithm selection constitutes a 
demonstrable and practically significant challenge within the Philippine 
academic and research landscape. 
The reviewed foreign literature further reveals a consistent limitation: 
although automated machine learning has advanced considerably, its 
capabilities remain oriented toward expert practitioners rather than novice 
learners. He et al. (2021) observed that  the usability of AutoML for non -
experts and the interpretability of its outputs remain limited, and Barbudo et 
al. (2023) formally characterized this accessibility gap as unresolved across 
eight years of AutoML  research. Even systems explicitly engineered for 
automation, such as the meta-learning-driven Auto-sklearn 2.0 of Feurer et 
al. (2022), prioritize predictive performance and hands -free operation over 

[PAGE 47]
 
 
 
 
the guided, interpretable explanation that a student requires. Rong et al. 
(2024) reinforce this observation, affirming that interpretable, user-centered 
explanation remains underdeveloped within contemporary machine learning 
systems. 
The local literature corroborates this gap within the Philippine 
context. The reviewed Philippine studies apply machine learning to specific, 
predetermined problems — household poverty classification (Salvador, 
2024), dengue forecasting (Francisco et al., 2024; Buebos -Esteve & 
Dagamac, 2024), urban temperature modeling (Gojo Cruz et al., 2025), and 
student-performance prediction (Švábenský et al., 2024) — each conducted 
by researchers already possessing machine learning expertise and each 
employing a fixed, pre-selected set of algorithms rather than guiding a user 
through informed algorithm selection. The Filipino -language natural 
language processing studies (Ramos & Vergara, 2023; Robantes et al., 
2024; Macrohon, 2024; Sagum & Sagum, 2025) further demonstrate that 
the interpretation of Filipino and mixed -language text remains a low -
resource and non -trivial undertaking — precisely the challenge that 
SimuRithm's NLP-based header and text-field analysis must accommodate. 
Across these works, no locally developed tool offers undergraduate 
students an accessible, dataset-driven pathway to algorithm selection. 
SimuRithm responds directly to this convergence of evidence by 
integrating dataset parsing, NLP -based header analysis, statistical feature 
extraction, and meta -learning-based recommendation within a unified, 
student-centered intelligent decision support system. By concurrently 
addressing the theoretical, technical, and pedagogical gaps identified 

[PAGE 48]
 
 
 
 
across both foreign and local sources, SimuRithm  occupies a role that no 
existing tool has yet fulfilled: the provision, to undergraduate students within 
the Philippine higher education context, of an accessible, guided, and 
educationally meaningful pathway toward informed machine learning model 
selection.

CHAPTER III 
METHODOLOGY 

[PAGE 59]
 
 
 
 
This chapter presents the methodology used in designing, 
developing, and evaluating SimuRithm : An Intelligent Decision Support 
System for automated machine learning algorithm selection. The methods 
described in this chapter were chosen to address the research questions 
stated in the Statement of the Problem, each of which required a specific 
approach, set of tools, and basis for evaluation. The organization of this 
chapter follows the input -process-output structure introduced in the 
Conceptual Framework, beginning with how data enters the system, 
followed by how the system processes it, and ends wi th how the system's 
outputs are measured and assessed. 
Method Research Used 
This study will use the developmental, experimental, and descriptive 
methods of research. The developmental method is suitable for this study 
because the main purpose is to design and develop a working system. 
SimuRithm will be created to help students analyze datasets and choose a 
suitable machine learning algorithm. The system will include features such 
as dataset upload, validation, NLP analysis, preprocessing, algorithm 
repository management, benchmarking, recommenda tion, visualization, 
and report generation. 
The experimental method will also be used because the study will 
test and compare different machine learning algorithms. The algorithms 
categories included in the study are classification, regression, clustering, 
anomaly detection, dimensionality reduction, topic modeling, text similarity, 
sequence modeling. These categories will be tested using selected 
datasets, and their results will be compared based on accuracy, precision, 

[PAGE 60]
 
 
 
 
recall, F1 -score, AUC, RMSE, MAE, R -squared, silhouette score, 
coherence score, execution time, and resource consumption. Through this 
method, the researchers will be able to determine how each algorithm 
performs under different dataset conditions. 
The descriptive method will be used to describe and evaluate the 
performance and quality of the system. The respondents will use SimuRithm 
and answer ISO/EIC 25010 criteria and usability indicator-based evaluation 
forms based on their experience. Their responses will help the researchers 
determine whether the system is useful, easy to understand, and helpful in 
reducing the trial -and-error p rocess in selecting machine learning 
algorithms.  
Research Design 
The study will follow developmental, experimental, and descriptive 
research design. The developmental part focuses on the SimuRithm system 
creation. The experimental component focuses on analyzing algorithm 
performance across different dataset types and NLP feature 
representations. The descriptive part focuses on evaluating user experience 
and software quality. 
The system will allow users to upload Excel or CSV datasets. After 
uploading, SimuRithm will validate the dataset and display the result. The 
system will then generate a dataset overview, apply NLP analysis to 
headers and text fields, display detected tokens and inferred roles, allow 
variable confirmation, perform processing, display a prepr ocessing log, 
generate candidate algorithms, run applicable algorithms, display 

[PAGE 61]
 
 
 
 
benchmark results, , compare algorithm performance using appropriate 
metrics, and recommend the most suitable algorithm with an explanation. 
The system will generate an exportable recommendation report containing 
dataset characteristics, preprocessing acti ons, benchmark results, and the 
final algorithm recommendation  
The Simurithm system will use an algorithm repository. Algorithms 
will be grouped by task and compatibility with data representation. For 
example, if the dataset has a categorical target, classification algorithms will 
be considered. If the dataset has a continuous target, regression algorithms 
will be considered. If no target is selected, clustering, topic modeling, 
anomaly detection, or similarity algorithms may be considered. If text fields 
are detected, NLP feature extraction will be performed before algorithm 
filtering. 
Locale of the Study 
The study will be conducted at the College of Computing Studies of 
Eulogio “Amang” Rodriguez Institute of Science and Technology, located in 
Nagtahan, Sampaloc, Manila. This location is suitable for the study because 
the target users of SimuRithm are stude nts from computing -related 
programs of the said school. 
Students from Computer Science and Information Technology 
programs are commonly exposed to programming, data analysis, and basic 
machine learning concepts. Because of this, they are appropriate 
respondents for evaluating a system that helps users understan d datasets 
and select suitable machine learning algorithms. The system testing may be 

[PAGE 62]
 
 
 
 
conducted in a computer laboratory or in any available academic setting 
where the respondents can access the system. It may also be conducted 
online, provided that all respondents are given the same instructions and 
evaluation forms. 
Respondents of the Study 
The respondents of the study will be undergraduate students from 
Computer Science and Information Technology programs. They will serve 
as the main evaluators of the system. The respondents will test SimuRithm 
and provide feedback about its usability, usefu lness, clarity, and 
effectiveness in helping users choose a machine learning algorithm. 
The respondents will be selected using purposive sampling. This 
means that the researchers will choose participants who are related to the 
purpose of the study. Since SimuRithm  is designed for students and 
beginner data analysts, the respondents must have basic knowledge of 
programming, datasets, or machine learning activities. The study may also 
include Computer Science or Information Technology faculty members as 
expert evalua tors, especially for checking the system based on software 
quality standards. However, the main focus of the evaluation will remain on 
the student respondents because they are the primary intended users of the 
system. 
Research Instruments 
The researchers will use several instruments to gather the needed 
data. An ISO/IEC 25010 -based evaluation form will be used to assess 
functional suitability, usability, performance efficiency, reliability, and 

[PAGE 63]
 
 
 
 
maintainability. Functional suitability will evaluate whether the system 
performs dataset upload, validation, NLP analysis, preprocessing, algorithm 
filtering, benchmarking, recommendation, visualization, and export. 
Usability will evaluate whether the sys tem can be easily understood by 
undergraduate students. Performance efficiency will evaluate whether the 
system processes datasets within acceptable time and resource limits. 
Reliability will evaluate whether the system handles invalid files, 
unsupported d atasets, and algorithm errors properly. Maintainability will 
evaluate whether the algorithm repository and NLP modules can be 
updated. 
A usability questionnaire will be used to measure whether users find 
the step-by-step outputs understandable, helpful, and easy to follow. A task 
checklist will be used to verify whether respondents can complete the main 
system tasks, including dataset upload, validation viewing, NLP output 
viewing, preprocessing review, algorithm comparison, recommendation 
reading, and report download. An algorithm performance record she et will 
store the metrics generated by each candidate algorithm. A dataset 
suitability checklist will be used to record file type, number of rows, number 
of columns, data types, text fields, missing values, target variable 
availability, and task type. A process-output checklist will be used to confirm 
that every major system step displays the required output. 
Data Gathering Procedure 
The researchers will collect sample datasets from public repositories, 
school-approved datasets, or instructor -provided datasets. Datasets may 
include numerical, categorical, alphanumeric, and text-based columns. CSV 

[PAGE 64]
 
 
 
 
and Excel files will be prioritized because these formats match the system 
scope. Each dataset will be uploaded to the system. SimuRithm will display 
an upload status and validation report showing whether the file type, 
structure, row count, column count, and readable fields are acceptable. 
The system will generate a dataset overview output that includes a 
preview table, data types, missing values, duplicate records, target 
candidates, and detected text fields. The NLP module will analyze column 
headers and text fields. It will display tokens , normalized terms, detected 
header meanings, possible variable roles, text -length summaries, 
vocabulary size, and suggested feature extraction methods. The user may 
confirm or customize the input variables, target variable, task type, and 
evaluation metric. The system will display the final variable selection output 
before preprocessing begins. 
The system will preprocess the data and display a preprocessing log. 
This may include missing value handling, encoding, scaling, tokenization, 
stop-word handling, vectorization, embedding generation, feature selection, 
and train -test splitting. The algorit hm repository will filter all applicable 
algorithms based on task type, dataset size, feature dimensionality, text 
representation, target type, and available computing resources. The system 
will display candidate algorithms and explain inclusion or exclusi on 
decisions. The system will execute the applicable algorithms, record 
performance metrics, and display benchmark tables and charts. It will then 
rank algorithms based on a composite score or user-selected priority. 
The recommendation engine will generate a final recommendation 
with an explanation. The results will include the best algorithm, alternative 

[PAGE 65]
 
 
 
 
algorithms, key dataset insights, performance evidence, warnings, and 
downloadable output report. Respondents will test the system and complete 
the evaluation of instruments. Their responses will be tabulated and 
interpreted using frequency, percentage, weighted mean, and qualitative 
comments where appropriate. 
Data Analysis Tools 
The researchers will use Python as the main programming language 
for developing and testing the system. Python is suitable for this study 
because it has many libraries for data analysis, machine learning, and 
visualization. Pandas and NumPy will be used fo r reading, cleaning, and 
processing datasets. Scikit -learn will be used to run the machine learning 
algorithms and compute their performance results. HTML will be used for 
the user interface of the system. Furthermore, JavaScript is employed within 
the HTM L structure to invoke and execute the Python code, enabling 
smooth interaction between the web interface and the underlying Python -
based functionality. This integration allows the webpage to communicate 
with Python processes, thereby supporting dynamic operations, data 
processing, and enhanced interactivity within the application. 
NLTK will be used for checking and processing the column headers 
of the dataset. This will help the system understand possible meanings of 
column names and support the system in identifying variables. Matplotlib or 
other graphing tools may be used to create charts, graphs, and other visual 
outputs. For the evaluation  of results, Microsoft Excel, Google Sheets, or 

[PAGE 66]
 
 
 
 
Python may be used to compute the weighted mean, percentage, and other 
needed results. 
The machine learning results will be analyzed using accuracy, 
precision, recall, F1-score, AUC, RMSE, MAE, R-squared, silhouette score, 
coherence score, execution time, and resource consumption. The 
evaluation responses from the respondents will be analyze d using 
frequency, percentage, and weighted mean. These tools will help the 
researchers determine the performance of the algorithms and the 
acceptability of the system. 
The system will utilize an SQL database to store historical records 
from the previous processes. The stored data can be retrieved and used as 
a reference for future operations. By maintaining the history, the system can 
be used for better support in decisi on-making, process efficiency, and 
support long-term operational needs.

This table shows the recommended metrics per category to ensure 
that machine learning algorithm are evaluated fairly and appropriately, 
SimuRithm will apply task-specific performance metrics based on the nature 
of the machine learning problem. Different al gorithm categories require 
different evaluation measures because a metric that is suitable for one task 
may not accurately reflect performance in another. Rainio and Klén (2024) 
emphasized that the selection of evaluation metrics should be aligned with 
the objectives and characteristics of the machine learning task being 
performed. 
For classification and classification-based sequence modeling tasks, 
the system will use Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, and Area Under 
the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). These metrics are 
widely used to evaluate the correctness of p redictions and the balance 
between correctly identified positive cases and classification errors. 

[PAGE 71]
 
 
 
 
Precision, Recall, and F1-Score are particularly important when dealing with 
imbalanced datasets where overall accuracy alone may be misleading. 
For regression tasks, the system will utilize Root Mean Squared Error 
(RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and R-Squared (R²). RMSE and MAE 
measure the magnitude of prediction errors, while R² indicates the 
proportion of variance in the dependent variable ex plained by the model. 
These metrics are among the most commonly accepted measures for 
evaluating predictive performance in regression models. 
For clustering algorithms, Silhouette Score will be used to assess 
cluster quality by measuring how well data points fit within their assigned 
clusters compared to neighboring clusters. Higher silhouette values indicate 
better-defined and more meaningful clusters. 
For topic modeling tasks, Coherence Score will be employed to 
evaluate the semantic consistency and interpretability of generated topics. 
Topic coherence has become one of the most widely adopted measures for 
assessing topic quality because it reflects the  degree to which topic 
keywords are meaningfully related. 
In addition to predictive performance metrics, SimuRithm  will record 
Execution Time and Resource Consumption for all algorithm categories. 
These measurements provide information regarding computational 
efficiency, allowing the system to recommend algorithms that not only 
achieve high predictive performance but also operate within practical time 
and resource constraints. This supports the study's objective of helping 

[PAGE 72]
 
 
 
 
undergraduate students select algorithms that are both effective and 
computationally feasible. 
Statistical Treatment of the Data 
The study will use frequency and percentage to describe the 
responses of the respondents. These will help show how many respondents 
selected a certain answer in the evaluation forms. The formula for 
percentage is: 
Percentage = (f / N) × 100 
where: 
f  =  number of responses 
N  =  total number of respondents 
The weighted mean will be used to interpret the results of the 
ISO/IEC 25010 evaluation and usability questionnaire. It will help determine 
the overall rating of the system based on the answers of the respondents. 
The formula for weighted mean is: 
Weighted Mean = Σwx / N 
where: 
w  =  weight of each response 
x  =  number of respondents who selected the response 
N  =  total number of respondents 
The following scale will be used to interpret the weighted mean: 

[PAGE 73]
 
 
 
 
Range Interpretation 
4.21 – 5.00 Strongly Agree / Excellent 
3.41 – 4.20 Agree / Very Good 
2.61 – 3.40 Neutral / Good 
1.81 – 2.60 Disagree / Fair

For the algorithm testing, accuracy will be used to measure how 
many predictions are correct. Precision will measure how many predicted 
positive results are actually correct, while recall will measure how many 
actual positive results are correctly identifi ed. The F1-score will be used to 
balance the results of precision and recall. Execution time will also be 
recorded to determine how long each algorithm takes to process the 
dataset. The following formulas will be used: 
Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN) 
Precision = TP / (TP + FP) 
Recall = TP / (TP + FN) 
F1-Score = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall) 
where: 
TP  =  true positive 
TN  =  true negative 

[PAGE 74]
 
 
 
 
FP  =  false positive 
FN  =  false negative 
Recommendation accuracy will also be used to check if SimuRithm  
recommends the correct algorithm. A recommendation will be considered 
correct if the algorithm suggested by the system is the same as the 
algorithm that gets the best result during testing. The formula is: 
Recommendation Accuracy = (Number of Correct 
Recommendations / Total Number of Test Datasets) × 100

Architectural Framework

Figure 3.10 Architectural Framework 
SimuRithm is structured around a layered architectural framework 
that organizes the system's components according to their role in the overall 
machine learning preparation and recommendation pipeline. The 
architecture is composed of five principal layers: the data intake layer, which 
handles dataset upload, validation, and parsing. The intelligence layer, 
which performs NLP -based header analysis, statistical feature extraction,

4.1.9 Handling Missing Values 
Statistics literature (Rubin, 1976; Little & Rubin, 2019) distinguishes 
three reasons why values go missing: 
 MCAR (Missing Completely at Random): the blank is unrelated to 
any value. Example: an encoder skipped S003's age by accident. 
 MAR (Missing at Random): the blank is explained by another 
recorded column. Example: study hours are missing more often for 
students with low attendance. 
 MNAR (Missing Not at Random): the blank depends on the 
missing value itself. Example: students with low previous grades 
chose not to report them. 
MNAR cannot be confirmed from the data alone, because the 
evidence is the missing values themselves. SimuRithm therefore assumes 
MCAR or MAR and applies simple single imputation, a common approach 

[PAGE 108]
 
 
 
 
for a quick, preliminary comparison of algorithms. Because simple 
imputation can bias results under MAR or MNAR, the blank counts are 
shown to the user in Phase 1 (Algorithm 4.7) before anything is filled. 
ALGORITHM 4.11  Handle Missing Values 
INPUT : table T, target y (may be absent), task k 
OUTPUT: table with no blanks, ready for training 
1  ▷ already done while reading (Algorithm 4.4): fully blank rows and 
columns removed 
2  IF k is supervised THEN remove every row whose y is blank      ▷ the 
answer is never invented 
3  IF k is supervised THEN split rows into training (80%) and test (20%) 
4  FOR each Numerical input column c DO 
5      m ← median of c over the training rows (all rows if there is no split) 
6      replace every blank in c by m 
7  FOR each Categorical input column c DO 
8      f ← most frequent value of c over the training rows; a tie goes to the 
first in sorted order 
9      replace every blank in c by f 
10 IF k ∈ {Topic Modeling, Text Similarity} THEN treat blank text as ""; 
drop documents left empty 
11 IF k = Sequence Modeling THEN remove every row that has any blank 
12 RETURN T

median is (19 + 20) ÷ 2 = 19.5, which fills S003. 
 Study_Hours: 4.5, 8.0, 10.0, 12.5 give a median of (8.0 + 10.0) ÷ 2

 Prev_Grade: 74, 79, 85, 88 give a median of 82.0, which fills S004.

4.1.11 Error Handling and Logging 
Each algorithm checks for failure at the point where it can occur, 
rather than relying on one generic error page: 
 Guard clauses stop a step as soon as a condition fails (Algorithm 
4.1, lines 1 to 7; Algorithm 4.4, lines 1 to 3). 
 Compensating deletion removes a stored file when its database 
record cannot be created, because the file system and the 
database cannot share one transaction (Algorithm 4.2, lines 7 and 
9). 
 Layered reply checks separate a service that is offline, a reply that 
is not JSON, and an HTTP error (Algorithm 4.3, lines 2 to 4). 
 Request validation in the analysis service returns HTTP 400 with a 
specific message (Algorithm 4.14); any unexpected failure returns 
HTTP 500 with its message. 
 Fault isolation lets one algorithm fail without stopping the others 
(Algorithm 4.15, lines 12 and 19). 
Every stage also writes a log entry, so a failed analysis can be traced 
to the exact step where it stopped.

over all rows, s = (b − a) ÷ max(a, b), where a is a row's mean distance to

SOP 3: How can a hybrid meta -learning or rule -assisted

Two properties follow. The score is relative: a 0.02 difference in 
accuracy spans the whole 0-to-1 range, so 1.000 means "best among these

4.3.4 Recommendation Output 
The engine returns the recommended algorithm, the average of its 
primary metric, its recommendation score, the task, a reason, and the full 
ranking. The reason is fixed per task, for example "Highest normalized score 
across accuracy, precision, recall, F1,  and available cross -validation 
results" for classification. Each result then receives its rank, and the winner 


[PAGE 137]
 
 
 
 
is flagged. The dashboard shows the recommendation in a card and places 
the winner first in the results table with a "Recommended" badge, and both 
reports include it. If tuning is on, the recommended algorithm is then tuned 
(Algorithm 4.24). In single mode only one algorithm is benchmarked, so line 
8 gives every metric 0.5 and the chosen algorithm is trivially recommended; 
a genuine comparison happens only when 3 to 5 algorithms are selected. 
4.3.5 Scope and Limitations 
1. Rule-assisted, not meta -learning. The engine ranks by 
performance on the uploaded dataset; it does not learn from past 
datasets which algorithm tends to win on similar data. 
2. Dataset and NLP features act before scoring.  They decide the 
suggested target and task and block unsuitable benchmarks, but 
they are not terms in Equation 4.8. 
3. Fixed weights. The user cannot set priorities such as "favor speed." 
4. Execution time is shown but not scored.  A model that is slightly 
more accurate but far slower still ranks first. 
5. Relative scores. Scores compare the current candidates only and 
cannot be compared across runs or tasks. 
4.3.6 Summary of SOP 3 
SimuRithm recommends algorithms with a rule -assisted, task -
specific, normalized weighted score (Algorithm 4.25). Dataset 
characteristics and header features shape the comparison; each metric is 
rescaled from 0 to 1 across the candidates and combined with fixed weights; 
and the highest-scoring algorithm is returned with its score, a fixed reason, 

[PAGE 138]
 
 
 
 
and the full ranking. All eight task categories receive a recommendation, 
and the recommended model can then be tuned.

Overall Discussion 
The prototype follows the study's Input–Process–Output framework: 
a CSV or Excel dataset and the user's confirmed choices go in; validation, 

[PAGE 148]
 
 
 
 
reading, type inference, profiling, header analysis, task detection, 
preparation, benchmarking, ranking, and tuning are performed; and a 
dataset profile, benchmark results, a recommendation with its reason, and 
reports come out. 
The five sample datasets show both what the reading procedures do 
well and where they fall short. Storage types were recognized in every case, 
including integer identifiers (D3), blanks that turn whole numbers into 
decimals (D1), and Excel dates (D5). Head er roles found the identifier in 
every dataset. Target and task suggestions, however, matched the intended 
setup only for D2, because the leftmost target word decides the target and 
tasks without a single target cannot be suggested. The two -phase design, 
in which the user confirms before any model is trained, is therefore essential 
rather than optional. 
The results should be read within the study's limitations. Header 
analysis is rule-based: it does not split camel-case names or reduce plurals, 
and it matches "id" inside any word. Task detection matches keywords 
inside words, so names such as Delivery_Tim e are misread, and its ratio 
rules depend on dataset size. Sequence modeling relies on the file already 
being sorted by time. Missing-value handling assumes MCAR or MAR data. 
Recommendation scores are relative and ignore execution time, and the 
engine does not learn from past datasets. Analyses longer than 300 
seconds, including tuning, are stopped, and English stop words leave 
Filipino function words in Taglish text.

Chapter Summary 
This chapter presented SimuRithm's techniques as Algorithms 4.1 to 
4.25 and traced them on five sample datasets. In Phase 1, uploads are 
validated, stored, and registered; the analysis service reads each file, infers 
integer, decimal, text, or date for eve ry column, profiles it, analyzes its 
headers, and suggests a target and task for the user to confirm. In Phase 2, 
the user's request is validated, the selected algorithms are benchmarked on 
identical data, ranked by a normalized weighted score, and optionally tuned, 
with feature importance as additional insight. Visible outputs at every stage 
and the PDF and Excel reports let students trace the recommendation back 
to their data. The ISO/IEC 25010 results will be inserted after the 
respondents complete the evaluation.
